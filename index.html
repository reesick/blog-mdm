1760 — 1840

STEAM,
COAL &
THE FACTORY
THAT ATE
THE WORLD

Before 1760, if you wanted a shirt, someone made it by hand. If you wanted iron, a blacksmith beat it out of ore, one piece at a time. The world moved at the speed of muscle and wind — and that was simply how things were.

Then James Watt refined the steam engine in 1769. It sounds like a minor technical upgrade. It was not. It was the moment humanity stopped being limited by the energy of living bodies and started harnessing something vastly, terrifyingly more powerful.

Steam engines ran at just 20–25% efficiency — wasting three-quarters of every unit of coal burned. And yet, they were still faster, stronger, and more tireless than any human or horse alive. The factory didn't just change how things were made. It changed where people lived, how families worked, and what a human life was worth in the market.

ERA 02
1870 — 1914
✦ UNLOCKED

THE WIRE
THAT LIT
UP THE
WORLD

Electricity changed everything — not just in factories, but in the fundamental texture of daily life. For the first time in human history, the workday was no longer bounded by sunlight.

Henry Ford's assembly line, introduced in 1913, took this power and mechanised human labour to a degree that would have seemed like science fiction to a worker from 1800. A Model T that once took twelve hours to build could now be assembled in ninety-three minutes. The world didn't just move faster — it moved at a pace that made everything before it look like standing still.

Industry 2.0 birthed the middle class. Mass production meant affordable goods. Affordable goods meant purchasing power. Purchasing power meant a new kind of worker — one who could buy what they built.

Pennsylvania Railroad's 1886 "Supplying Department" — the first formal procurement organisation in history — appeared in this era. Even the way companies bought things had to be reinvented.

CLASSIFIED DETAIL

Ford's 1913 assembly line cut Model T build time from 12 hours to 93 minutes. The same car, the same workers — but the system had been fundamentally rewired.

ERA 03
1960s — 2000s
✦ UNLOCKED

THE MACHINE
LEARNED
TO THINK

The first two revolutions replaced human muscle. The third one went further. It began replacing human decision-making — at least, the repetitive, rule-bound kind.

Programmable Logic Controllers arrived in 1969. For the first time, a machine could be told: "if this condition is true, do this." Robots entered factory floors not as novelties but as the cheapest, most reliable workers money could buy — no breaks, no unions, no errors.

The internet arrived and rewired global commerce. Freemarkets.com launched the first digital reverse auctions in 1995, changing how corporations sourced everything from raw materials to components. The supply chain — invisible to most people — became the circulatory system of the global economy.

But here was Industry 3.0's silent price: it displaced workers faster than it created new jobs. Factories that once employed hundreds ran with dozens. Entire skilled trades — tool-and-die, machining, precision assembly — began to vanish. Progress, yes. But progress that left a human cost in its wake.

CLASSIFIED DETAIL

Industry 3.0 introduced the first online reverse auctions via Freemarkets.com (1995) — suddenly, every supplier on earth was competing in real time. Price transparency hit like a shockwave.

ERA 04
2010s — PRESENT
✦ UNLOCKED

EVERYTHING
CONNECTED.
EVERYTHING
TALKING.

Industry 4.0 didn't just upgrade the factory — it dissolved its walls. When Rolls-Royce fitted IoT sensors to jet engines so they could predict failures before they happened, the factory floor had effectively expanded into the sky above every commercial flight on earth. The concept of "local" manufacturing ceased to mean anything.

BMW introduced collaborative robots — cobots — that worked side by side with humans on the production line. GE deployed digital twins: full virtual replicas of physical machines, running in parallel, predicting failure and optimising performance in real time. DHL built smart logistics networks where every package knew where it was and when it would arrive before a human looked at a screen.

And yet — Industry 4.0 had a dirty secret that McKinsey eventually quantified: fewer than 10% of companies attempting advanced process control actually extracted strong value from it. BCG confirmed that only 5% of firms piloting AI initiatives achieved meaningful returns at scale.

The machines were talking to each other. But nobody had stopped to ask: were humans still part of the conversation? Operators became passive monitors. Skills were trained around tools rather than judgment. The efficiency metrics looked incredible. The humans running the systems felt increasingly like spectators in their own factories.

Industry 4.0 connected everything. It just forgot to ask who it was all for.

ERA 05
2021 — THE FUTURE
✦ UNLOCKED

THE MACHINE
FINALLY
CAME HOME

In 2021, the European Commission did something unexpected. It didn't announce a new technology. It announced a new purpose. Industry 5.0 was formally defined not by what machines could do — but by what they should do, and for whom.

The three pillars: Human-centric. Sustainable. Resilient. After 260 years of asking "how fast can we go?" — Industry 5.0 finally asked "but should we?"

Mercedes-Benz, building the S-Class, made a decision that would have been unthinkable in 4.0: they deliberately reduced automation. Because the dexterous, judgment-rich process of assembling a luxury vehicle couldn't be done better by a robot than by a skilled human. The machine stepped back. The craftsperson stepped forward.

Nike combined AI systems with human designers — not to replace creative judgment, but to augment it. The AI handles the data, the iteration, the options. The human decides what's beautiful, what's meaningful, what tells a story worth wearing.

This is the defining insight of Industry 5.0: the most powerful factory is not the most automated one. It is the one where technology frees human creativity to be its most human.

THE THREE PILLARS
I
HUMAN-CENTRIC

Workers are assets, not costs. Technology serves people — not the other way around.

II
SUSTAINABLE

Circular economies. Waste converted to resource. UN SDGs baked into manufacturing DNA.

III
RESILIENT

Supply chains that bend without breaking. Systems designed for crises, not just calm days.

The Industry 5.0 market is projected to reach $129.1 billion. Personalisation and sustainability are no longer nice-to-have — they are the competitive moat.
