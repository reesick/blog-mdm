ERA 01
1760 / 1840
✦ UNLOCKED

STEAM,
COAL &
THE FACTORY
THAT ATE
THE WORLD

Before 1760, if you wanted a shirt, someone made it by hand. If you wanted iron, a blacksmith beat it out of ore, one piece at a time. The world moved at the speed of muscle and wind, and that was simply how things were.

Then James Watt patented his separate-condenser steam engine on 5 January 1769.[1] It sounds like a minor technical upgrade. It was not. It was the moment humanity stopped being limited by the energy of living bodies and started harnessing something vastly, terrifyingly more powerful.

Watt's 1769 separate-condenser engine was barely 2–3% thermally efficient,[1] but it cut coal use by roughly three-quarters versus Newcomen's earlier design. And yet, even that small flame was faster, stronger, and more tireless than any human or horse alive. The factory didn't just change how things were made. It changed where people lived, how families worked, and what a human life was worth in the market.

The Luddites were not anti-technology. They were skilled weavers who understood exactly what was coming. Between 1811 and 1816, they smashed the frames not out of ignorance but out of despair. A craft that had taken a lifetime to master was being replaced overnight, at a fraction of the cost, by a machine that needed no sleep, demanded no wage, and felt nothing.

By 1840, Britain was producing half the world's iron and cotton. Manchester's population doubled, then doubled again. A rural England that had existed for centuries dissolved in a single generation, not gradually but in a series of shocks that remade the social fabric of an entire nation. The steam engine did not merely power the factories. It powered everything that came after.

ERA 02
1870 / 1914
✦ UNLOCKED

THE WIRE
THAT LIT
UP THE
WORLD

Electricity changed everything, not just in factories, but in the fundamental texture of daily life. For the first time in human history, the workday was no longer bounded by sunlight.

Henry Ford's assembly line, introduced in 1913,[1] took this power and mechanised human labour to a degree that would have seemed like science fiction to a worker from 1800. A Model T that once took twelve hours to build could now be assembled in ninety-three minutes.[1] The world didn't just move faster, it moved at a pace that made everything before it look like standing still.

Industry 2.0 birthed the middle class. Mass production meant affordable goods. Affordable goods meant purchasing power. Purchasing power meant a new kind of worker, one who could buy what they built.

Pennsylvania Railroad's 1886 "Supplying Department",[2] the first formal procurement organisation in history, appeared in this era. Even the way companies bought things had to be reinvented.

Tesla and Edison fought a war over the future of electricity — AC versus DC. Edison's direct current was powerful but limited in range. Tesla's alternating current could travel hundreds of miles. The stakes were not just commercial but civilisational. AC won, and with it came the modern grid, the modern city, and the modern notion of a fully electrified life. Industry 2.0 did not just mechanise production. It rewired the world.

ERA 03
1960s / 2000s
✦ UNLOCKED

THE MACHINE
LEARNED
TO THINK

The first two revolutions replaced human muscle. The third one went further. It began replacing human decision-making, at least, the repetitive, rule-bound kind.

Programmable Logic Controllers arrived in 1969.[1] For the first time, a machine could be told: "if this condition is true, do this." Robots entered factory floors not as novelties but as the cheapest, most reliable workers money could buy, no breaks, no unions, no errors.

The internet arrived and rewired global commerce. Freemarkets.com launched the first digital reverse auctions in 1995,[2] changing how corporations sourced everything from raw materials to components. The supply chain, invisible to most people, became the circulatory system of the global economy.

But here was Industry 3.0's silent price: it displaced workers faster than it created new jobs. Factories that once employed hundreds ran with dozens. Entire skilled trades, tool-and-die, machining, precision assembly, began to vanish. Progress, yes. But progress that left a human cost in its wake.

The ERP system — enterprise resource planning — became the nervous system of the corporate world. SAP, founded in 1972, eventually ran the back-end of 77% of the world's transaction revenue. Every purchase order, every inventory count, every payroll run funnelled through its logic. The computer had not merely entered the office. It had become the office.

ERA 04
2010s / PRESENT
✦ UNLOCKED

EVERYTHING
CONNECTED.
EVERYTHING
TALKING.

Industry 4.0 didn't just upgrade the factory, it dissolved its walls. When Rolls-Royce fitted IoT sensors to jet engines so they could predict failures before they happened,[1] the factory floor had effectively expanded into the sky above every commercial flight on earth. The concept of "local" manufacturing ceased to mean anything.

BMW introduced collaborative robots, cobots, that worked side by side with humans on the production line. GE deployed digital twins: full virtual replicas of physical machines, running in parallel, predicting failure and optimising performance in real time. DHL built smart logistics networks where every package knew where it was and when it would arrive before a human looked at a screen.

And yet, Industry 4.0 had a dirty secret that McKinsey eventually quantified: fewer than 10% of companies attempting advanced process control actually extracted strong value from it.[2] BCG confirmed that only 5% of firms piloting AI initiatives achieved meaningful returns at scale.[3]

The machines were talking to each other. But nobody had stopped to ask: were humans still part of the conversation? Operators became passive monitors. Skills were trained around tools rather than judgment. The efficiency metrics looked incredible. The humans running the systems felt increasingly like spectators in their own factories.

Industry 4.0 connected everything. It just forgot to ask who it was all for.

The data centre became the new factory floor. By 2020, the world was generating 2.5 quintillion bytes of data every day. Every sensor reading, every machine handshake, every logistics ping added to a torrent of information that no human could read but algorithms could devour.

The cloud wasn't a place. It was a new kind of industrial infrastructure, as essential to 4.0 as coal was to 1.0.

Supply chains became hyper-optimised. A car built in Germany pulled parts from 300 suppliers across 30 countries, coordinated by software that recalculated in real time. Just-in-time was no longer a philosophy — it was a mathematical constraint enforced by code. The factory had become a node in a planetary network.

ERA 05
2021 / THE FUTURE
✦ UNLOCKED

THE MACHINE
FINALLY
CAME HOME

In 2021, the European Commission did something unexpected. It didn't announce a new technology. It announced a new purpose. Industry 5.0 was formally defined not by what machines could do, but by what they should do, and for whom.[2]

The three pillars: Human-centric. Sustainable. Resilient. After 260 years of asking "how fast can we go?", Industry 5.0 finally asked "but should we?"

Mercedes-Benz, building the S-Class, made a decision that would have been unthinkable in 4.0: they deliberately reduced automation.[3] Because the dexterous, judgment-rich process of assembling a luxury vehicle couldn't be done better by a robot than by a skilled human. The machine stepped back. The craftsperson stepped forward.

Nike combined AI systems with human designers, not to replace creative judgment, but to augment it.[4] The AI handles the data, the iteration, the options. The human decides what's beautiful, what's meaningful, what tells a story worth wearing.

This is the defining insight of Industry 5.0: the most powerful factory is not the most automated one. It is the one where technology frees human creativity to be its most human.

The pandemic proved the point violently. Supply chains built entirely on algorithmic optimisation — lean, efficient, globally distributed — shattered under the first real shock in decades. Resilience, the third pillar, turned out not to be a soft idea. It was the hardest engineering requirement of all.

Industry 5.0 is not a rejection of 4.0. It is its correction. The sensors stay. The data flows. The algorithms still run. But at the centre of the system now sits something that no machine has ever been able to replicate: the human who asks why.
